Here you’ll find my research and teaching.

I am a DPhil student in Mathematics at the University of Oxford and a member of Balliol College. My interests lie in topological data analysis, algebraic topology, and mathematical biology, with a particular curiosity about the connections between mathematics and medicine.

My route into mathematics began in medicine. Alongside my medical training, I studied mathematics and physics at Utrecht University, completing master's degrees in all three subjects. That background continues to shape the questions I find interesting: how can mathematical structure help us understand complex biological systems?

My supervisors include Helen Byrne, Alain Goriely, Lida Kanari, and Heather Harrington. Previously, I worked with Ard Louis at Oxford on simplicity bias and sloppiness in gene regulatory networks.

Alongside my DPhil, I work with Medscio BV, a Dutch medical startup working to help medical record systems communicate with each other.

As Scott Alexander put it:

“The utopia where all hospitals use the same electronic medical record system, or at least medical record systems that can talk to each other, so that doctors can look up what the doctor you saw last week in a different hospital decided instead of running all the same tests over again for $5000.”

— Scott Alexander, Meditations on Moloch (2014)

Topological Data Analysis / Demos

Topological data analysis

Topological data analysis (TDA) uses tools from algebraic topology to study different kinds of data, including point clouds and branching trees. Here are some demos to show how these tools can be used.

Point clouds: the Vietoris–Rips complex

What am I looking at?

This is the 2-skeleton of a Vietoris–Rips complex. An edge joins points at distance at most ε; a triangle is filled when all three edges are present. The displayed counts are computed from the complex at the current scale.

β₀ counts connected components. β₁ counts independent unfilled loops, using homology over ℤ₂. Higher-dimensional simplices do not change these two counts. This is an illustration of the filtration used in persistent homology; it does not compute a persistence barcode.

The point clouds are synthetic examples. Distances are in arbitrary units.

TMD: from a tree to a barcode

Lida Kanari and colleagues’ Topological Morphology Descriptor (TMD) summarises a branching tree as a persistence barcode. These barcodes can then be used for tasks such as classifying neuronal cell types. Compare these two reconstructed cells, then move the slider to follow their branching structure.

How is the barcode computed?

Starting at the tips, walk back towards the root. At each junction, keep the component with the greatest distance to its tip. Each other component gives a bar from that junction to its tip. The surviving component gives a bar from zero to the furthest tip.

Here distance is length along the 3D branches, measured from the apical arbor’s root. The drawings are 2D projections. Colour and numbers link each apical branch to its barcode; basal dendrites appear in grey. The shared slider cuts at the same percentage of each arbor’s maximum path length. These are biological example reconstructions, not assigned cell-type labels.

Morphologies: NeuroTS example reconstructions. Barcodes computed with TMD 2.4.3.

Kanari, Dłotko, Scolamiero et al. (2018). A Topological Representation of Branching Neuronal Morphologies. Neuroinformatics 16, 3–13. TMD software ↗

Cell-type classification: Kanari, Ramaswamy, Shi et al. (2019). Objective Morphological Classification of Neocortical Pyramidal Cells. Cerebral Cortex 29, 1719–1735.

TNS: from a barcode to a tree

A synthetic tree is analysed with TMD, then its barcode is used by NeuroTS to generate new trees. These three independent runs were computed locally with the authors’ software. Play or scrub through all three together to compare their growth from the same input.

SomaComplete tree

Generated with NeuroTS 3.7.0; the barcode was extracted with TMD 2.4.3. Each run grows an apical dendrite in 3D, shown here in a 2D projection. The slider shows the same percentage of each run’s recorded growth steps, not biological time. Download the trees (SWC): seed 137, seed 271, seed 459.

What is being reproduced?

TMD uses path distance along the source tree here; the cell comparison also uses path distance. NeuroTS receives the source barcode and associated branch-angle information, together with the authors’ example growth settings. It samples growth, bifurcations, and terminations. The result need not have exactly the same barcode or branching structure, so TNS is a stochastic approximate inverse rather than a unique reconstruction.

The source tree was also generated with NeuroTS, using the authors’ example inputs. It is not a biological reconstruction. All three output recordings use its extracted barcode; only the random seed changes.
